Medicine

I have always liked science, even though I studied the liberal arts at university and took no science in my final two years of high school. This personal statement should give you an idea of how and when I rekindled my passion for science, and how I realised that medicine was what I wanted to pursue as my vocation.

I wrote this personal statement as part of my application to study medicine at Otago University in New Zealand. I was unsuccessful because I did not meet the eligibility requirements (gah!). Nonetheless, I enjoyed the process of putting my thoughts and beliefs about medicine and science into words. I hope you enjoy reading it as much as I enjoyed writing it.

At first glance, choosing to study medicine may seem like an unusual choice for me. I haven’t formally studied science since I was fifteen, I am working in a non-scientific job, and I am still young and very early on in my career. Yet, I believe my experiences of medicine - though fleeting - in combination with my own independent research and reflection have given me enough information to be sure that I am committed to studying medicine as my vocation - a fierce passion that also happens to be a job.

I am coming to medicine via a roundabout path. At school, I never considered medicine because I did not study any science subjects past Year 11. I went into university frankly unsure of what I would do afterwards. It was only in my second year of university that I first seriously considered the idea of pursuing medicine. I liked the idea of being able to enter a field that was both intellectual and practical. I also liked the idea of being able to work closely with people and to make a meaningful difference to people’s lives. But I was not certain, and I decided to contact several hospitals to see if I could get some clinical shadowing experience.

In a week during my Christmas break, I was fortunate enough to shadow a number of physicians and surgeons across internal medicine, surgery, pathology and general practice. This was a formative experience for me. I found I enjoyed the outpatient clinics of the breast surgeon I shadowed the most. I enjoyed learning about people’s lives and getting an insight into how they tick – some were witty and wisecracking, others a little more reserved and wary. It was instructive to watch how the surgeon I shadowed adapted her approach depending on the patient: some people responded well to gentle banter, whereas others needed more explicit reassurance. Although I didn’t realise this at the time, I later realised what a privilege it was for a patient to invite me into their lives and share their vulnerabilities and struggles with an absolute stranger. I think there are very few jobs in the world that can match those moments of open, honest, and genuine connection.

But I also witnessed some unhappy moments. One that stuck in my head was from a morning shadowing a pathologist: I watched as she and her fellow consultant were called in to analyse a frozen section and became visibly upset as they saw the telltale signs of glioblastoma in a fellow doctor. Even though I did not know what glioblastoma was at the time, the reactions of the consultants in the room immediately told me that something was very wrong. That experience was particularly moving for me because it reminded me that for all the science that underpins medicine, it is fundamentally about people. It also taught me that modern medicine has its limits.

Even after shadowing, I had doubts about entering medicine. One of my biggest worries was that it is easy to have a romantic view of medicine, and a week of shadowing is not nearly enough to learn about the more challenging aspects of the job – complications and M&M meetings, frustrated and anxious relatives, working while tired and stretched and hungry – among other challenges. To rectify this, I scoured the internet and asked some of my medical friends for medical book recommendations. I read these voraciously, hoping to inoculate myself with a more realistic view of medicine. I read and reread all of Atul Gawande and Henry Marsh’s books, in addition to memoirs by Paul Kalanithi and Rachel Clarke, and more specialised books such as Graham Easton’s excellent quasi-non-fictional book about being a GP. I was pleased that, far from being put off from medicine, I became more fascinated and intrigued by it – as well as more aware of the painful consequences when things inevitably don’t go to plan. I particularly enjoyed Henry’s piercing honesty about his work and his willingness to admit his mistakes to a fault. That kind of vulnerability was a side of medicine I had not learned from shadowing. But I also realised that, for all the suffering and grief that occurs in medicine, that is precisely what makes it correspondingly rewarding and meaningful work.

Even though it’s been a long time since I formally studied the sciences, I’ve come to realise that I still love these subjects. In preparation for the GAMSAT – the Australian medical school entrance exam – I self-studied chemistry, biology and physics by using online resources and textbooks. Working through these alongside my final university exams was genuinely enjoyable, and reminded me just how much I enjoyed learning science in school. It was great to see my hard work pay off with a score of 87 in the science section. But I know that I still have many gaps in my scientific knowledge. If I’m fortunate enough to receive an offer from Otago, I’ll be looking to reinforce my existing knowledge and reteach myself any missing knowledge to the best of my abilities so that I am ready to hit the ground running on day 1.

I’ve come to medicine via a roundabout path, but I am sure that this is what I want to do as my vocation. To me, there is no career quite like it: the ability to support someone in what is likely to be one of their most vulnerable moments, while also being able to marshal all the scientific knowledge and discoveries at our disposal to try to help them.

Update, Sep 2026: Four months after I applied to Otago University, I received and accepted an offer to study medicine at Sydney University. I'm very excited about the opportunity to study a subject I find utterly fascinating (I've already got started with pre-learning course content!) and to pursue a career I feel very strongly is my calling in life - no apologies for the cliched expression. Sydney is also my home town, and I'm looking forward to being a more dutiful son and a more present friend. It'll be good to be back.
